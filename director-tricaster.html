Before you touch anything

All four cameras have to be up on the CCUs or the switcher will not come up at all. This rig has no partial failure — it is a whole show or nothing. One bad cable takes everything. Check the signal-up gate on the Today tab before doors, not at kickoff.

The switcher is the last thing in the chain. If a camera is missing here, the problem is almost never the switcher — it is a cable, a converter, or a camera that has not been brought up. Say what is missing on comms rather than hunting through menus.

The two rows

Two rows of buttons run the show. Program is what is going out right now. Preview is what goes out next. You set up on Preview and then move it to Program.

  • Program (top row) — live. Pressing a button here cuts straight to air with no warning to anyone.
  • Preview (second row) — safe. Nothing here is on air. Set your next shot up and look at it before you take it.
Work on Preview. The habit that separates a calm show from a messy one is never touching the Program row during a play. Preview, look, take.

Take, Auto and the T-bar

  • Take — a hard cut. Instant. This is most of football.
  • Auto — runs the selected transition over its set duration. Dissolves between wide shots, into replays, out of a video.
  • T-bar — you drive the transition by hand. Useful when you want to hold halfway or match a moment.

Cut on action, dissolve on a pause. During live play, cut. Between plays and into graphics, dissolve. A dissolve mid-tackle looks like a mistake; a cut into a slow stadium wide does too.

DSK layers

DSK stands for downstream key — a layer sitting on top of Program. Scorebug, lower thirds, sponsor bugs. There are four, DSK 1 to DSK 4.

  • Each DSK has its own Take and Auto so you can bring a graphic on without touching the picture underneath.
  • DSKs sit downstream of everything, so they stay up through a camera cut. That is the point of them.
  • A progress bar under each DSK group shows whether that layer is on, off, or mid-transition.

Delegates — the one that catches people

If Take or Auto seems to do the wrong thing, check your delegates first. This is the single most common source of "the switcher is broken".

The main Take, Auto and T-bar act on whichever layers are delegated to them — the background, or one or more DSKs, or several at once. Click a layer's monitor to delegate it, or use the BKGD and DSK buttons in the Transition Delegate group on the panel.

So if you press Auto and the camera does not change but a graphic disappears, the T-bar was delegated to a DSK and not to the background. Nothing is wrong; it did exactly what it was told.

Look Ahead Preview respects your delegate selection, so what you see is what the next take will produce.

M/E buses

An M/E is a small switcher inside the switcher. Build a shot there — a split screen, a picture in picture, a camera with an effect — and then select that whole M/E as a single source on Program or Preview.

This machine has four M/E rows plus PREVIZ. PREVIZ lets you build and check a layout without it being visible anywhere, which is the right place to prepare something mid-show.

DDR and media players

  • DDR 1 and DDR 2 — video playback. Hype videos, sponsor spots, the Texas Way video, drone shots.
  • GFX — graphics pages.
  • Media plays from the point the playhead is at. Reset it before doors or the first roll starts halfway through.
  • A DDR can be set to stop or loop at the end. Getting this wrong is how a sponsor spot plays twice.
Watch the countdown under the player. The run of show gives you the duration; the countdown tells you the truth.

Macros

A macro fires a sequence with one button — take a camera, bring up a DSK, roll a DDR, all together. The panel has a HIT MACRO key.

Macros are worth building for anything you do the same way every week: the open, the anthem, going to break. They remove the chance of doing step two before step one at the moment you are being talked to on comms.

FTB and getting out of trouble

  • FTB — fade to black. The safe exit when something is wrong on air. Use it rather than cutting around looking for a clean shot.
  • Undo exists on the switcher and covers most accidental changes.
  • If a source shows black, check the camera and the cable before the switcher. See the note at the top of this page.

Our setup

  • Four cameras plus a spare. Camera 1 blue, 2 red, 3 green, 4 yellow — the same colours as the CCUs and the switcher markers, the roster, the checklists and the stadium maps. Not the tally lights — those are all red.
  • XPN on the switcher is the XPression graphics machine.
  • Encoder 1 goes to YouTube. Encoder 2 feeds the stadium monitor that crew and offices watch on their phones.
  • The monitor feed carries whatever encoder 2 is pointed at, and its audio. Changing that source changes what everyone sees.
Camera names on the multiview are people, not positions. They go stale the moment someone swaps. Naming them by position and colour instead would match every other document in this system — worth doing before someone calls for a name that is not there tonight.

LivePanel on the TriCaster

The TriCaster serves its own web page on the stadium network. Open it from any phone, tablet or office machine on site. It carries the built-in training and documentation for the exact software version installed — under Resources — which is more reliable than anything on the open web.

Open LivePanel

LivePanel is control, not viewing. It can switch sources, change audio and fire macros on a live switcher. Read the training in it whenever you like; do not touch Switcher or Audiomixer during a show unless you are the one directing.
  • Only works on the stadium network. It will not open from home.
  • If it does not load, the TriCaster's address may have changed — it is assigned by the district and is not guaranteed to stay the same.
